Multi-Agent Framework for Decentralized Peer-to-Peer Energy Orchestration with Satellite-Informed Demand Forecasting
 
Abhiraj Bambhore
School of Computer Science and Engineering
REVA University
Bangalore, India
abhirajbambhore@gmail.com


Abstract— Rooftop solar generation in India reached approximately 20.8 GW of cumulative installed capacity by end-2025, yet grid operators were forced to discard an estimated 2.3 TWh of that energy while simultaneously paying out an estimated ₹6,000 crore in must-run compensation — not because demand was absent, but because neighboring households had no way to trade directly. Traditional auction systems using cloud technology and public-chain blockchains cannot fulfill the latency and privacy requirements of residential trading. The paper presents a complete local-edge energy trading system where an autonomous multi-agent engine negotiates real-time prices, forecasts household supply using satellite-informed agent predictions, matches buyer and seller bids within physical grid capacity constraints, and permanently records each settlement using a dual-tier local blockchain. Simulation results show that the approach reduces wasted solar energy by 20% and improves cluster efficiency by 22.5%.
Keywords— Peer-to-Peer Energy Trading, Multi-Agent Systems, Satellite Imagery, Physics-Based Validation, Blockchain, Edge Devices.
I.	INTRODUCTION 
India’s cumulative rooftop solar capacity reached approximately 20.8 GW by end-2025, yet the electricity grids serving those homes were never designed to move power sideways between neighbors. When a household generates more than it needs at midday, that surplus has nowhere local to go — grid operators absorb it as a liability rather than an asset. In 2025, there was 2.3 TWh of solar energy discarded and ₹6,000 Crore paid out in must-run compensation to those generators whose output could not be absorbed by the grid. Distributed agent-based scheduling has been shown to recover significant wasteful flexibility [1],[2], and structured peer-to-peer trading has provided tangible efficiency gains at the neighborhood level [3].
The gap between neighbors that need to buy and sell surplus energy from each other is easy to understand, but hard to fill: there is currently no infrastructure that allows them to do so in a timely manner using existing assets [17]. Centralized auction systems typically require internet connectivity and can be as slow as 500 ms, which is incompatible with real-time residential scheduling requirements and the data-minimization obligations of the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023. Also, the public blockchain based systems will typically have a consensus delay of between 12 and 30 seconds — again, much too long for a scheduling cycle of 15 minutes. The systems that use a hybrid of agent negotiation and smart contracts still depend on the external infrastructure and ignore the physical safety of the grid. To date, there are no deployed technologies that combine speed, privacy, safety, and affordability into one system.
This paper presents an architecture that meets all four criteria. Each home has a PZEM-004T meter and a ESP32, which provide real-time readings to a cluster server at the community level. The cluster server performs all functions locally, including agent-based price negotiation based on satellite solar forecasting, validation of the physical grid using the IEEE 33-bus network model, and settlement of transactions through a blockchain - all within each 15-minute time period without leaving the local area network. Section II surveys related work; Section III presents the system architecture and methodology; Section IV reports simulation results; and Section V states conclusions.
II.	LITERATURE SURVEY
A major concern with residential energy management is whether or not intelligent agents can coordinate energy usage in a neighborhood without any one party having control over the entire process. The authors of the study conducted by Li et al. have demonstrated through computer simulation that household agents are able to negotiate schedules for the use of appliances with each other, thereby achieving near-optimal energy allocation on both the supply and demand sides of an electricity grid. The researcher's finding that their models and results remain stable under degraded conditions of the electricity grid further corroborates that agent-based coordination can succeed at managing the unpredictable solar energy production commonly experienced in Indian housing clusters.
Lum et al. approached this challenge differently by addressing the coordination problem in energy flows among distributed sources and loads using a contract-net protocol to negotiate instead of relying on a central coordinator [2]. This aspect of their work is relevant since it demonstrated this type of negotiation scales as additional agents are added, which will be necessary for any neighborhood-scale deployment. The present system's bid-offer structure between Supply Agents and Demand Agents is based on this bidding protocol.
Paudel and Gooi created a two-dimensional trading model for a community-based microgrid in which prosumers create energy contracts using bilateral transactions that are mediated by a central node [3]. Their experiments with this structure produced significantly improved results for the ways they used energy, thus confirming that this kind of structured negotiation between prosumers is worth exploring further. However, they do not provide any privacy for household-based consumer consumption data or the ability to use their proposed model without a single point of contact for communications. This makes their design inappropriate for the Indian regulatory situation.
The Baig et al. team was among the first to create a functional physical model of a peer-to-peer energy marketplace, using Internet of Things (IoT) meters and cryptocurrency backed smart contracts to facilitate transactions without any intermediaries [4]. The ability to test their ideas using real equipment added a level of validity to their project that simulation-based studies do not possess. Their finding — specifically that public blockchain transaction verification latency prevents real-time scheduling of residential electricity use — is the same issue the two-tiered local ledger structure used in this project has been designed to resolve.
Armenia and Gavrilaş designed a local electricity market where agents negotiate prices autonomously and transactions settle via Ethereum smart contracts, with a pricing formula specifically designed to favour higher trade volumes and benefit smaller households [5]. It is the prior work most closely related to this paper in scope and intent. The difference is that their system routes all settlement through cloud-hosted blockchain infrastructure and makes no attempt to verify whether proposed trades are physically safe for the grid — two omissions that the architecture described here directly addresses.
Forecasting accuracy on Indian grid data is addressed by Shetty et al., whose hybrid deep learning and Particle Swarm Optimization model achieved a 3.69% mean absolute percentage error on an urban grid digital twin [6]. That level of accuracy on India-specific data justifies using a similar deep learning architecture for the demand forecasting layer here. A separate but related question is how to run such forecasting without exposing household data. Kutseva et al. examined machine-learning approaches for residential energy forecasting and cost optimization [7], demonstrating that collaborative model training can preserve data locality — an important foundation for the federated approach adopted here.
Whether commodity hardware can run a serious energy management system is answered by Bojović and Bojović, whose IEMS integrated hybrid inverters and storage using off-the-shelf components and demonstrated real microgrid performance [8]. This is important background for a system running on a mini-PC. The question of whether optimal power flow can be computed locally rather than on a cloud server is answered by Reddy and Vignesh, whose graph-theoretic OPF with ADMM across a cyber-physical grid achieved lower costs and better reliability than centralised control [9]. Xie et al. extend this picture to task scheduling, showing that edge devices in smart grids can sustain reliable local computation with no cloud dependency at all [10].
Hariprasad et al. presented an adaptive hybrid optimisation model for siting renewable energy sources and EV charging stations in smart grids under demand uncertainty [11]. Their EV charging deadline constraint formulation directly informed the bid structure of the Demand Agent in the proposed framework. Zhang et al. reviewed market power detection methods in local flexibility markets and developed a multi-agent reinforcement learning environment for prosumer interaction simulation [12], highlighting the importance of anomaly detection in agent-based trading — addressed in the present system through the Governance Agent's constraint-violation scrubber.
Shimada and Takeuchi showed that medium-resolution Sentinel-2 satellite imagery is sufficient to reliably detect rooftop solar PV installations without needing costly high-resolution aerial data [13]. This finding is what makes satellite-only sensing a viable input for the Solar Availability Index used in the proposed vision layer. Li et al. have presented evidence that utilizing hierarchical multi-agent systems using cooperative agents to coordinate their activities can decrease the electricity consumed as well as prevent grid overloads ([14]). This supports our decision to use the actor model. Chen reached a similar conclusion from the fault protection angle, finding that distributed agent topologies outperform client-server designs in real-time microgrid scenarios [15]. Finally, P. D. Bojović and Ž. Bojović extended their earlier energy management work to cover distributed resource coordination across multiple physical nodes [16], offering the closest hardware-level precedent for the multi-node prosumer cluster that future deployments of this system will require.
III.	METHODOLOGY
At its core, the system is a five-layer decentralised architecture operating entirely over a local area network. Each prosumer home contributes an ESP32 sensor interface, and a PZEM-004T Class 0.5 energy meter, all connected to a shared Beelink SER5 cluster server within the neighborhood. The only time the technology requires internet access is to download the Sentinel-2 tiles periodically. All of the negotiation, inference, settlement and explanation logic operates locally within the requirements of India's DPDP Act 2023 data-minimization regulations. The five layers of the architecture form a sequential pipeline: satellite sensing and federated edge learning, context aggregation, multi-agent negotiation with physics validated, cryptographic settlement with XAI on-device and prosumer dashboard for interaction.
 
Fig. 1.	Proposed five-layer decentralised P2P energy orchestration architecture
As depicted in Fig. 1, Layer 1 uses Sentinel-2 imagery with computer vision processing and federated learning nodes with local home sensors. Layer 2 aggregates context models (model weights only, no raw data). Layer 3 runs Supply, Demand, and Governance agents with optimal power flow validation, and a Reasoning and Trust layer with a local large language model and dual-tier blockchain. Layer 4 provides a real-time interactive web dashboard for prosumer interaction.
A.	Data Source and Privacy Layer at the Edge
Layer 1 pulls Level 2A multispectral imagery from the ESA-Copernicus open-access API. A computer vision pipeline calculates band ratios corresponding to aerosol optical depth and cloud fraction, which together produce a Solar Availability Index —a value between 0 and 1 — that estimates how much solar energy each household cluster can expect to generate over the next 15-minute window.
The homes utilize local demand forecasting models based off of historical consumption of the home’s PZEM meters in conjunction with the satellite pipeline. Raw consumption data is never sent out from the device. Only the mathematical gradients generated by the training are sent to the aggregation server located on the Beelink SER5 device and they are averaged together from all homes participating in order to update the global model. Each home’s usage patterns are completely private at all times throughout this process.
B.	Input Aggregation Layer
Each time a new cycle of negotiations starts, four streams of information are sent to the aggregation layer: the most current SAI reading; a demand forecast from every registered home; battery data from BMS interfaces; and EV charging due dates provided by demand nodes. The four streams create a single unified context snapshot in which agents can assess whether to bid or make an offer by ensuring their offers and bids represent the current physical state and the forecast for that cluster at that time.
C.	Governance and Agentic Negotiations
There are three agents running in parallel on the cluster server. The Supply Agent tracks the SAI feed and, when a household forecasts surplus energy, sends out an offer for that surplus energy with a price and volume for sale. The Demand Agent monitors for urgent consumption needs, such as an EV charging deadline or a depleted home battery, and sends out a time-sensitive bid for electricity. The third agent, Governance, acts like a physics referee. Before any matched trade gets onto the ledger, it performs an optimal power flow analysis based on the IEEE 33-bus network model using the actual line impedances and transformer ratings of the cluster. Additionally, only trades that keep the node voltages within the 0.95–1.05 per unit band and keep the thermal loadings of each line within the safe operating limits are accepted. If a trade is rejected due to breaching one or more of these constraints, that trade is recorded with the constraints that were violated as part of the explainability layer.
D.	Dual-Tiered Blockchain Settlement
Settlement is conducted in two phases on the cluster server without involving an outside network. Each trade that is approved can be recorded in real-time on a lightweight hash chain ledger called the L2 layer. This ledger allows for the immediate creation of new blocks (less than one second), allowing for complete real-time negotiation. Each transaction entry has an identifiable digital signature from both prosumers to ensure that each prosumer has an undeniable track of who made what trade. Periodically, batches of L2 records are forwarded to an L1 smart contract on a local Ethereum node hosted on the same server. From there, DISCOMs read a standardised trade event stream to reconcile accounts. Running both ledger tiers locally means no gas fees, no public network delays, and no household data leaving the building — while still satisfying the auditability requirements of government compliance.
E.	Explainability on The Device
Every trade record in the ledger carries a short plain-language explanation generated by a large language model running on the cluster server. A prosumer checking why their offer was rejected might read that cloud cover was 72% at the time, their battery was already at 84%, and the trade would have pushed the local transformer past its thermal limit. There is no use of a cloud service - everything that the user processes happens in their local hardware environment, so the user does not need to have any prior expertise in understanding the output/data. The intention of this model is that residents have sufficient trust in the system due to having enough transparency provided to them, even without an engineering background.
F.	Proposed Hardware Solution
Table I depicts a set of the planned physical hardware for the system, which is expected to include commercially available components that are sized for home use. While the proposed system has not yet been built, the performance measures described in Section IV come from software simulations. The physical prosumer node is made up of  an ESP32-WROOM-32, a PZEM-004T v3.0 energy meter, and a 4-CH relay module. The physical Beelink SER5 Mini PC hosts all agents and performs several functions: it serves as the shared cluster edge server; it runs a local blockchain node that supports all smart contracts in the energy market; and it runs one of the on-device large language model (LLM) inference engines. All devices are networked through a TP-Link EAP225, and the network between devices is Wi-Fi, but only the Internet is used for occasional downloading of Sentinel-2 satellite tiles.

IV.	RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
The evaluation is framed by three metrics. Orchestration Efficiency Gain (OEG) measures how much better the cluster manages its total energy cost relative to a no-trading baseline. Solar Curtailment Reduction (SCR) captures how much surplus solar energy is prevented from going to waste. Federated Learning Convergence (FLC) tracks forecast accuracy after a fixed number of collaborative training rounds. Across a 15-node simulated cluster in Bengaluru, OEG reached 22.5%, SCR reached 20%, and the shared demand model achieved a mean absolute error of 0.18 kWh per 15-minute window after five federated rounds — all without any raw household data leaving individual devices.
To determine the performance of the proposed system, we compared it against multiple baseline and state-of-the-art techniques including centralized double auction platforms, pure blockchain P2P systems and MAS frameworks without physical-world constraints. Table II shows that the proposed system outperforms both baseline approaches across every measured dimension. The 22.5% orchestration efficiency gain exceeds the centralised double auction by 8.3 percentage points and the blockchain P2P baseline by 5.7 percentage points — a difference attributable to the combination of satellite-informed proactive offer generation and physics-constrained trade approval, neither of which is present in the baselines. The 44-fold reduction in trade latency relative to blockchain-only settlement (340 ms versus 14,200 ms) is a direct consequence of replacing public network consensus with a local hash-chained ledger, enabling the system to operate within the 15-minute scheduling window. The OPF governance layer, absent in all prior approaches reviewed, independently rejected 3.2% of trades that would have induced transformer overloads or voltage violations — demonstrating that safety-unconstrained P2P systems carry a non-trivial grid risk that this architecture explicitly addresses.

Fig. 4. Negotiation Efficiency: Price Settlement vs. Utility Rate
Fig. 4 depicts trade settlement prices over 50 consecutive simulated P2P trades (using a representative two-month input window) plotted with their corresponding ₹8.50/kWh reference grid rate. All 50 trades settled at prices that fell between ₹6 - ₹8 per kWh (a range that provided greater benefit to the seller than having gone unsold plus, also provided greater benefits for the buyers than had they purchased from the grid). The green shaded area between the trade prices and the utility rate line denotes the cumulative benefit experienced by all buyers participating in this trading activity. Therefore all 50 transactions being located within this shaded area indicates that the agent engine successfully delivers mutually advantageous arrangements between buyers and sellers.
As illustrated by Fig. 4 and 5, the time necessary to perform vision analytics (0.15 seconds) and simulate a double auction (0.05 seconds) is insignificant compared to the times required for LLM negotiations (4.2 seconds) and generating an XAI explanation (2.8 seconds). The combined pipeline average, including OPF validation and communication overhead, is approximately 7.5 seconds per residential electricity trade, comfortably within the 15-minute scheduling window.

All five performance dimensions (efficiency improvement, decrease in curtailed energy use, trading delay, accuracy of forecasts using federated learning, and accurate detection of clouds) consistently demonstrate that the architecture described can work for a residential setting in the real world. The federated learning model reached an MAE of 0.18 kwh without leaving the local network for any raw household data, complying with the DPDP Act 2023 requirement and allowing the model to have comparable accuracy to that which would be achieved from a centralised system. Additionally, the precision for the Vision Agent was greater than 0.95 at recall levels of up to 0.45, which will produce a statistically significant number of solar availabilities regardless of the amount of cloud cover present during a specific time period. Thus, together, the results of this study support the theoretical possibility of using this solar design in a cooperative housing society in Bengaluru as a fast follow-up to the envisioned construction of such a project.
V.	CONCLUSION
This paper aims to provide a solution for an issue with current systems of energy trade - allowing neighbouring households the ability to trade surplus solar energy on an ongoing basis, with a high level of privacy, security, and low cost. The 5-Layer architecture proposed in this paper achieves this by executing agent-based negotiations, forecasting demand, checking the OPF grid for compliance, settling the trades on the blockchain and providing users with an explanation of each transaction entirely within a neighborhood LAN environment without any transmission of data to third parties outside the local network environment. Across 9,000 simulated scheduling rounds conducted on the IEEE 33-bus standard test network, cluster efficiency improved by 22.5% and solar curtailment was reduced by 20% relative to the no-trading baseline.
In the service of meeting all the requirements for data minimization as prescribed by the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, all transactions are carried out offline - every single one, thus no household electricity consumption history will ever leave the consumer's premises. This eliminates all costs and delays associated with using gas or cloud networks. This next phase will involve the establishment of five to ten property level prosumer nodes at an apartment complex in Bengaluru, allowing us to compare actual consumption versus real and non-uniform load conditions on those nodes to be able to make adjustments accordingly. The next stage will include integration with a move towards India’s Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC, the Digital Rupee), allowing for both sides of each transaction to settle simultaneously rather than rely on payment processes carried out across public networks.
